Before the numbers

Eleven hours and fifty-five minutes.

That is the whole distance now. One night, one aircraft, no connection.

You leave Heathrow in the afternoon and you sleep badly, the way everyone does. Then the cabin lights come up over the Andaman Sea and you land into a morning that is already twenty-nine degrees and smells faintly of rain on hot concrete. A driver is holding a card with your surname on it. That is the last decision you have to make for five days.

The building is on Sukhumvit, eight storeys, glass and white render, a garden on the roof with the Bangkok skyline behind it. Inside it is cool and quiet and smells of nothing at all, which is what a good clinical building smells like. Somebody takes your blood before nine. By lunchtime a doctor has your numbers in front of her and is deciding what happens on Tuesday.

On day three they drive you to the coast, because you cannot run a protocol like this and then sit in a meeting. On day five you fly home with a report and a slightly irritating habit of waking before your alarm.

This is not a spa. It is also not an ordeal. It is five days in which somebody competent takes the wheel.

None of that is an argument against the NHS. In an emergency it is one of the finest systems on earth, and if something is actually wrong with you, that is exactly where you should be. It was simply never designed for the well-but-declining man in his fifties, and it has never pretended to be.

So you went private and met the second version of the same thing. A morning of tests, a letter, a leaflet about walking more. An industry whose product is information about you — paid the same whether it finds something or nothing, and with nothing to sell you when it doesn't.

And underneath both, the shelf. Seventy pounds a month of magnesium and NMN. A ring that grades your sleep out of a hundred. A subscription to watching the problem.

Half a million Britons a year have already stopped waiting. Most of them went for teeth, or hair, or a new hip. Almost nobody is going for the thing that would actually help at fifty-one.

The mechanism

The Bio-Reset Sequence

Eleven appointments across five days. Nine of them inside the first forty-eight hours. Four steps, in a fixed order, none longer than an hour, nearly all of it lying down.

  1. Pressure

    Hyperbaric oxygen chamber. You lie down, the chamber is pressurised, you breathe.

  2. Light

    Full-body red light therapy, two sessions, on a clinical panel array rather than a spa bed.

  3. Clearance

    Medical colon hydrotherapy, performed inside the hospital by its own staff.

  4. Fuel

    High-dose intravenous nutrition, or PMST bodywork. The doctor chooses which on day one, from your labs.

The claim is the order and the calendar, not the machines. Every one of these exists in London and you could book all of them yourself. You would get the chamber on a Tuesday in March, your bloods back three weeks later, the drip in May if you remembered to rebook. Four months to half-finish a sequence that is only a sequence when it runs across five consecutive days in one building.

It isn't the equipment you're missing. It's the diary — and a place where all of it is on the same floor.

Before you ask

The questions everyone sends back

Is this a spa week with a stethoscope?

No, and if that is what you want you will be disappointed. Day two is five appointments back to back, including colon hydrotherapy. There is a doctor on intake, a laboratory, and a written biomarker review before you fly. Days three and four are genuinely a holiday, deliberately.

Can my wife or husband come?

Yes, and most people do. They are welcome on the transfers and the coastal days. Clinical appointments are per person — if they want the programme too, tell us during the consultation and we can discuss the appropriate plan for them. Thailand's medical visa route is designed for companions to stay for the whole trip.

What if I am on medication, or have something ongoing?

Say so on the enquiry. A doctor reviews every intake before anything is confirmed, and some people are told this is not appropriate for them. This programme is preventative and restorative — it is not treatment for a diagnosed condition, and it does not replace the care of your own GP or consultant.

How far in advance should I book?

Four to six weeks is comfortable, mostly for the flight. There are fifty-eight beds in the building and only a small number of international programmes run at a time, so the constraint is real rather than a sales line.

What actually happens after I send the form?

A person telephones you on the number you give, at a UK-friendly hour. Bangkok is six hours ahead, so expect the call in your morning. You get the full itinerary, the medical intake questions and a written quote. No deposit is taken on that call.

Who am I dealing with?
